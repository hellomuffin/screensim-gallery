ScreenSim

A deterministic screen-world simulator and a user-in-the-loop benchmark for real-time assistance — the complete setting, two benchmarks, and all results.

ScreenSim is a white-box simulator of an iPhone whose entire world is a deterministic state machine, paired with an interaction benchmark in which a simulated human performs a phone task while an AI assistant watches the rendered screen and may speak but never touch the device. The engine injects realistic mistakes at task subgoals and holds the human on the flawed course; the assistant is scored on whether it detects the mistakes, stays silent when nothing is wrong, and helps the human recover before the outcome is irreversible. This document gives the setting, the two benchmarks built on it — a single-error benchmark and a multi-error benchmark whose every episode carries ≥2 injected errors — and the full results, including a ten-way model leaderboard. It is written for a methods section and appendix; implementation is intentionally omitted.
world: 5 apps · 51+ screens · 20 widget kinds tasks: 11 long-horizon episodes: 30 multi-error, dual-audited models: 10 tested · 3 personas · 1,080 runs

1Overview and contributions

Real-time assistance is the task of a bystander who can see and speak but not act: it is judged not only by catching errors but by the restraint to stay quiet, and by timing against an irreversible outcome.

Most agent benchmarks put the model in control of the world and score the final state. ScreenSim inverts this: the model is an advisor to a human who holds the controls. The contributions:

  • A white-box screen world (§2–3): a deterministic state machine whose ground truth — what is on screen, what a control does, whether the goal is met — is exact and machine-checkable, never inferred from pixels or an LLM judge.
  • Anchored, certified error injection (§6): each mistake is a single realistic slip attached to a task subgoal, with a certified point of observability and of consequence, admitted only after dry-replay.
  • A soft-hard takeover (§5) that makes the injected error actually happen and releases control only on a correct, accepted intervention — so detection is always measurable and credit is attributable.
  • A multi-error benchmark (§10) whose every episode carries two or more errors of different types, each verified non-conflicting and independently audited by a vision model and a separate agent.
  • A ten-way leaderboard (§11) across hosted and open-source vision-language models and three personas, with asymmetric, timed metrics that reward restraint and punish irreversible harm.

2The environment

A single seed fixes the entire phone, so any two runs of the same task face an identical world.

Definition 2.1 — World
A world is a tuple W = (S, φ, s₀, τ).
S a tree of application state (the scene graph) φ the projection S → screen (visible screen + widgets) s₀ the seeded initial state (deterministic in the seed) τ the transition function τ : S × gesture → S (total, deterministic)

The scene graph S is ground truth; the visible screen φ(S) is a projection. Agents observe only a rendering of φ(S); the grader reads S.

A screen is a named node (settings.sdp, bank.amount) holding ordered widgets; a widget has a kind, a human-readable label, an optional value, and an affordance (the transition it triggers). Widget kinds fall in three groups — the distinction matters for the error taxonomy, because only widgets that write committed state can carry a forceable error.

groupkindsrole
value controlstoggle, radio, cc_round, cc_vslider, cc_tilewrite a durable setting when actuated
navigating / committing rowsrow, account, tile, listitema tap navigates and may also commit (a payee pick)
input & inertfield, button, section, text, banner, image, icon_button, toolbtn, usagebar, homesearch, cc_mediatext entry, actions, non-interactive structure
Table 2.1 — The 20 widget kinds. Row-type widgets both navigate and commit, which is why a wrong-payee tap is a genuine forceable error (§6).

Every affordance names a verb — ~40 in all, split into navigation (change screen, no write), reversible commit (a toggle, a radio, a payment source), and irreversible commit (pay, place_order, send_mail, verify_otp). Irreversible verbs write protected paths IRR = {bank.history, bank.balance, shop.order, mail.sent}; these define the deadline the assistant races. Because τ and the renderer are deterministic in the seed, an episode is fully reproducible from (seed, task, error, persona, assistant) — the property every controlled comparison here relies on.

Full coverage. Every row in Settings opens a page that looks and behaves like the real one: 60 screens are hand-written from the User Guide, and the remaining 346 (every row that once led to a placeholder, down to the leaves) are synthesized from their label, their parent page and the guide's own "Settings > … >" steps — a description line and a few working switches, option lists, value rows or sub-pages, in the register of iOS 18. Their state lives apart from every task's state, so a wrong turn can land anywhere and the person still sees a realistic phone, while no goal or injected error can depend on a synthesized control.

3Observation and action

The assistant sees what a person over a shoulder sees; the human acts one gesture at a time, named by the words on the screen.

3.1 Observation tiers

Each turn the assistant receives the last k=2 rendered frames of φ(S), oldest to newest — never the scene graph, affordances, screen keys, or any grading signal.

tierobservationpurpose
sv1rendered frames onlythe honest default — perception under test
sv2frames + a relational text mapa perception ablation

3.2 Action space

Definition 3.1 — Gesture grammar
tap <label> actuate the widget labelled ⟨label⟩ swipe up | swipe down scroll type "<text>" enter text into the focused field press back | press home pop the stack | return to the launcher

Grounding is semantic (by visible label), not spatial. The engine is an affordance gate: a gesture runs iff its target is present, visible and interactive; otherwise it is refused with a reason and the state is unchanged. Navigation is therefore a real part of every task — and a real place for error.

4Tasks and their subgoal decomposition

A task is a goal a competent person completes in one sitting; the suite favors long-horizon tasks that cross several screens, because each transition is a place a mistake can happen.

Definition 4.1 — Task
A task is T = (ι, g, π, seed): an instruction, a machine-checkable goal predicate g:S→{0,1}, a reference plan, and a seed.

The instruction ι is the only goal knowledge either agent receives; the plan π is engine-internal. The reference plan is lifted into ordered subgoal nodes of two kinds — commit (writes durable state) and reach (a required forward navigation) — and the progress key (the committed-state diff sequence, restricted to plan-touched paths) is path-invariant: two routes to the same outcome yield the same key.

5The interaction protocol

Two language models sit behind a strict one-way channel; between them the engine runs a soft-hard takeover.

5.1 Two agents, channel-isolated

The human agent role-plays the person whose hands move the phone; the assistant agent watches and may only talk. The human sees the goal, the live screen, its own history, the assistant's spoken lines, its own history, and its private persona (skill, trust); it emits {utterance, move, close_conversation, plan_update} — only utterance crosses. The move is one of the seven CookSim conversational moves (ask_why, question_correctness, ask_for_more_guidance, ask_for_less_interruption, accept, reject, answer), and the plan_update (drop | append | replace + gestures) is how a convinced person re-plans. The assistant sees the goal, the User Guide procedure for the task (the Apple iPhone User Guide article sections that name the task's controls, from the same local mirror the tasks were authored from; the simulator's own apps supply their help text), the last 2 screenshots, and its own previous reply; it emits {task_progress, goal_change, text, flag} — only text crosses. task_progress is the assistant's own subgoal ledger (completed / current / future); the flag is null or {error: t} with t one of the four taxonomy names in §6, graded by exact match (the spoken text is only secondary credit). The manual is what lets an advisor know which row is right; without it every task would be a guessing game. A prompt audit (which exempts the quoted manual) fails the run if the assistant prompt contains the plan, the slip, a detection window, a grading term, or a coordinate: the assistant must infer both the correct steps and any mistake from the goal and the pixels alone. The human acts mostly in silence — a real person does not narrate each tap — speaking only to ask, react, flag, or announce a change.

5.2 The soft-hard takeover

A benchmark that merely asks the human to err is unsound: a compliant model may follow the assistant's clean guidance and never make the mistake. The error is instead the engine's doing.

  1. Advance. The person plans its own taps, a few at a time, from the goal and what it sees — nobody hands it the steps; if it does not know where a setting lives it asks the assistant. The engine watches each tap the person is about to take.
  2. Hard takeover (inject). When the tap the person is about to take is the designed error's anchor step (same effect on the world, on the screen the step belongs to), the engine executes the designed slip instead of that tap; the mistake now exists in the world and the person believes it made it.
  3. Hold. While the slip stands, the engine blocks the person's own attempts to undo it (a value cannot be set back, a wrong page cannot be left) — so the assistant, not luck, must cause any recovery. A commit is never held back: an unrepaired error commits.
  4. Soft release. A flag with the correct type arms the error; only the person's accept move releases it. The person then states its fix as a plan_update; the fix is dry-run on a copy of the phone and carried out only if it actually undoes the slip (a human fix), otherwise the released body undoes it the canonical way (a fallback repair, counted separately). A late acceptance routes back to the screen the slip happened on, undoes it, and returns.
  5. Maximum hold. If no correct detection arrives, the error stands — a reversible one persists to the final state, an irreversible one commits.
Why this shape. The error is guaranteed to occur (detection is always measurable), self-recovery is suppressed (credit is attributable), and release is conditioned on a genuinely correct intervention (no credit for a vague or lucky remark). The takeover applies to every human: in the multi-error benchmark the person is always an LLM (gemini-3.7-flash), never a script.

6Human-error taxonomy

Every deviation is either a mistake the assistant must catch or a legitimate change it must not flag.

6.1 Forceable errors

An error family is forceable if the engine can realize it by substituting a single gesture at the anchor — a positive wrong action, not an absence. Only forceable families are admitted, because only they can be guaranteed under the takeover.

familydefinitionexample
wrong_valuea sibling option of the same control — right control, wrong valuePay Alex Kim instead of Alex Chen.
wrong_targeta different control on the same screen — the intended one is left unsetToggle Notification Center instead of Control Center.
wrong_pageat a required navigation, a plausible wrong turn to the wrong screenOpen Control Center settings while seeking an app's privacy page.
extra_writean additive foreign write — an extra actuation the task never asked for, on a goal-relevant path the plan carries only from a defaultAlso tap "Visa · 3% fee", paying from the wrong source.

6.2 Benign deviations (the assistant must not flag)

Restraint is half the benchmark. An intent episode has the human announce a deliberate change of plan and adopt it as the goal; a clean episode has no deviation at all. The announced intent is the hardest restraint case — it looks exactly like a wrong_value error but is legitimate.

Omissions are out of scope, by design. Two omission families — skip_step and early_commit — were considered and removed. An omission is the absence of an action, which the takeover cannot force: a free human who wanders off simply never produces the mistake, yielding an unscoreable run. Restricting to forceable families is what makes injection guaranteed and grading honest.

6.3 Certification

A candidate error is admitted only if dry-replay shows: the transformed plan runs without refusals; it does not still satisfy the goal; the deviation is the sole difference from the reference outcome; a validated recovery exists that restores the reference world (not merely one that satisfies the goal — a wrong toggle must be switched back, a moved radio re-selected, an extra choice undone, before the correct control is taken); and the anchor does not collapse to t₀ unless it is genuinely the first committed choice. Certification yields an observability point and an outcome point — the two ends of the detection window.

7Personas

The person is drawn from the CookSim persona-v2 roster: a private card on two axes.

Skill (novice / average / expert: how well they know the phone) and trust (low / medium / high: how readily they believe a correction) are the whole persona. Both are private: the person plays them, and the assistant is told nothing about the person — it must read skill and trust off behaviour. There are no mechanical dials and no questionnaire: a low-trust expert rejects because it is playing a low-trust expert, and a high-trust novice accepts a wrong correction for the same reason.

8Evaluation metrics

The assistant is scored on three axes — detection, restraint, and timing — and any episode where the designed mistake did not actually happen is voided, so no metric is credited to an event that never occurred.

8.1 Detection verdicts (single-error)

correctnamed the injected error (or its in-window equivalent)
early_correctcorrect, before the error was strictly observable
neutrala true statement about the world, not the injected error
false_alarmneither the error nor true of the world — a restraint failure
misspresent, never flagged
Table 8.1 — A detection counts as correct iff its verdict is correct or early_correct — one definition, shared by the takeover-release rule and every scorer.

10Benchmark B — multi-error, dual-audited

Every episode carries two or more errors of different types at distinct subgoals, verified never to conflict, and independently audited twice.

10.1 Composition and certification

Eleven hard tasks host composite episodes — 30 in all (three per task where the interface affords three genuinely different mistakes; the two payment tasks and the StandBy task afford one or two). The hard set is the original five (phone lockdown, app quarantine, readability, lock-screen lockdown, rent share) plus six added in the second wave: a hearing set-up (LED flash, mono, captions; VoiceOver must stay off), a profiling opt-out (tracking, analytics, Siri improvement; iCloud analytics must stay on), a lock-screen Siri lockdown (Control Center must stay on), a keyboard clean-up (spell check and auto-capitalisation must stay on), a bedside StandBy set-up (Night Mode must stay on), and an instant landlord payment. Every task pairs three to six commits across one to three settings trees with a look-alike neighbour and a "keep X" constraint.

A composite admits ≥2 errors of ≥2 different kinds only if: the anchors are distinct; the perturbed paths are disjoint; no two errors act on the same control (a self-cancelling pair); the combined plan dry-replays without refusals and leaves the goal unmet; each committing error breaks the goal on its own (so no error masks another); and each error's repair, spliced right after it, restores the reference world. Errors must be plausible: a wrong turn lands on a page the plan never visits and is the nearest such row; a wrong choice is within a row or two of the right one or shares a word with it (the two Alexes); an unwanted extra is a control the instruction told the person to leave alone — the authored traps ("Access iCloud Data on the Web", "Check Spelling", "Control Center", the 1–3-day payment option). Episodes within a task are distinct by the setting each committing error breaks and the step of each wrong turn, never merely by which decoy row was tapped. Episodes take three shapes — nav→slip, two committed slips, and slip + unwanted extra.

10.2 The dual audit

Every episode is verified by two independent auditors:

  • VLM audit — a vision model inspects the rendered screen after each injected error and judges whether the mistake is visible and meaningful. Result: 60 / 60 errors (the pass is run twice; a single unstable judgment on the first pass — "the toggle did not change" on a switch that started on — did not recur and was also overturned by inspection of the frame).
  • Subagent audit — a separate agent reads each episode's plan and frames and judges whether it is executable, semantically meaningful, and non-conflicting. Result: 30 / 30 episodes, after three audit rounds: the first two rounds failed episodes for near-duplication (the same wrong turn with a different decoy row), for implausible slips (a stranger two rows below the right payee), for wrong turns onto pages the plan visits anyway, and for a suite that never exercised the instruction's own trap — each finding became a designer rule above, and the whole set was re-derived and re-audited until every episode passed.
The audit found real defects. The two auditors caught bugs a purely mechanical pass admitted: additive errors that flipped a task target (a twin); a settings screen whose four rows collapsed to two state variables (a twin); a wrong-toggle error with no validated repair; and a wrong choice that re-used a plan target and silently self-cancelled. All four were fixed and re-verified — the three-track method (mechanical + vision + fresh reader) is what made the episodes trustworthy.
A second wave, found by watching the videos. Reviewing the filmed episodes exposed four more defects the audits' single frames could not: (i) twenty-three settings rows shared a state path with another row (tapping one silently flipped the other), fixed engine-wide by giving every row its own path and re-deriving the auto-generated task set; (ii) the additive error had no undo, so an accepted detection "repaired" nothing; (iii) repairs were validated on the goal alone, so a wrong toggle stayed switched on after the "fix"; (iv) prevention was read off the error's own state path, which a commit can reset. The cures are the counterfactual grading of §8.2, the world-restoring repair rule of §6.3, and two standing checks: an error-free completion of every task (the engine-correctness baseline) and a validation harness in which the oracle must prevent every error and win every episode while the silent floor must commit every error that fires — the error designs themselves were unchanged, so the audits stand.

11.2 Findings

  • Two errors remain much harder than one. On the expanded 30-episode benchmark no model reaches F₁ 0.6 against the oracle's 1.0, and the best completes fewer than half of the episodes (S 0.48) — models fixate on the first mistake and miss the second (cf. the single-error detection of 0.92 in §9). The ceiling is now a true 1.0 on every persona: the human pauses for one last look before putting the phone down, so a last-step error is as preventable as any other.
  • The best open model beats the best hosted one. qwen2.5-vl-32b leads (F₁ 0.58, S 0.48) ahead of gpt-5.4-mini (0.51, 0.42) and qwen-27b (0.41, 0.28), and it does so with acceptable precision (0.85) — but it talks on almost every turn (CC 0.02): reach bought with a running commentary.
  • Within a family, bigger is not better. gpt-5.4-mini beats gpt-5.5 (F₁ 0.51 vs 0.28) and gemini-3.7-flash beats gemini-3.1-pro-preview (0.29 vs 0.23) — the larger hosted models over-flag (P 0.30 / 0.35, an alarm most turns).
  • The open-source scale curve. Across qwen2.5-vl 3B→7B→32B recall climbs 0.12→0.22→0.57 while the model goes from near-silent (CC 0.97) to talking every turn (0.02); precision stays high (1.00→1.00→0.85). Restraint is what the small models have and the large one spends.
  • Two opposite failure modes. gpt-5.5 and gemini-3.1-pro-preview over-flag (≈1 false alarm per episode); the small open models under-flag — near-silent, near-perfect precision, and they miss most errors. minicpmo is the quiet exception: precision 0.98 at recall 0.36, almost never speaking (CC 0.96) yet catching a third of the errors — the most economical assistant on the board.
  • The skeptical persona is hardest for everyone. brisk_expert (pushes back once) halves every real model's success (gpt-5.4-mini 0.60 → 0.20; qwen 32B 0.67 → 0.30) while the oracle still scores 1.0 — a genuine user-model effect, not a harness artefact.
  • Tasks differ sharply. For gpt-5.4-mini, F₁ ranges from 1.0 (the lock-screen Siri task) and 0.78 (keyboard) down to 0.19 (readability) and 0.0 (the instant landlord payment): the payment errors — a wrong payee, a card source, a slow speed option — are the ones assistants miss, and they are the irreversible ones.

12Validity and reproducibility

A run that measured nothing is voided, not scored — this is what keeps every reported number honest.

gatevoids when…
slip_not_conductedthe human never made the designed slip
injection_never_firedthe deviation never became observable
error_had_no_effectthe error neither committed, was repaired, nor changed the outcome
run_stalledthe run ended on a loop / no-progress guard
no_verdict_reachedno detection verdict could be assigned

Determinism (§2) makes every episode reproducible from its factors, so an ablation that varies one factor holds the rest of the world identical. The silent floor and oracle ceiling are engineered brackets present in every run, so a score has meaning on an absolute scale — and they double as the engine's acceptance test: a benchmark build is admitted only when the oracle prevents every error and wins every episode, the silent floor commits every error that fires, and every task completes error-free on film (engine-correctness baseline).

13What the appendix should enumerate

All of the following are exact and machine-generated; the engine recomputes them:

  • Per-task scene walk — the reference plan, every screen it passes through, and the subgoal nodes with their committed paths.
  • Full error & composite ledger — every candidate error and composite, admitted or rejected with the admissibility check it failed.
  • Composed prompts — the verbatim human and assistant prompts, with the channel-isolation contract and the prompt-audit term list.
  • Per-episode records — injection / observability / outcome ticks, verdict, latency, recovery, and any validity gate.
  • Per-persona & per-model breakdown — the 450-run leaderboard split by persona, plus the two audit ledgers (VLM per-error, subagent per-episode).
